The structure of the script

How Ndebe Works

Build a syllable from its parts, read its tone, and see how the numbers fit together.

Syllables

Ńdébé represents syllables. In a consonant–vowel syllable, the stem and radical form the consonant body, and the vowel marker sits on the connecting branch above it. The marker tells you both the vowel and its tone.

There are 6 stems and 7 radicals. Their combinations make 42 consonant bodies. Nine vowels each have three tone forms: high, mid and low, giving 27 vowel markers.

The dotted shapes are teaching placeholders: a tall rectangle for a stem, a small square for a radical, and a horizontal rectangle for a vowel. They show where a component belongs.

A vowel on its own

A vowel that forms its own syllable uses its standalone form. The extra dot beneath distinguishes it from a vowel attached to a consonant body.

N / M is Special

N/M can form a syllable without an accompanying vowel. This syllabic nasal carries its own tone and uses one of three standalone forms. It does not go on top of a consonant body like a vowel marker.

These three N/M forms are additional to the 27 vowel markers. When n or m is the consonant of a consonant–vowel syllable, use the appropriate consonant body with its vowel above.

Numbers

Ńdébé numbers use base 20, with a sub-base of 5. There are 20 digits, from 0 to 19. Larger numbers use place value; each place to the left is worth twenty times the one beside it.

Twenty digits from a small set of forms

Within a digit, the body contributes 0–4 and a flag contributes 0, 5, 10 or 15. Adding a flag to the bodies for 1–4 gives 6–9, 11–14 and 16–19. The digits 5, 10 and 15 have a plain body with no extra units.

Place value beyond nineteen

The places are ones, twenties, four hundreds, eight thousands, and so on. Zero holds an empty place. The examples below give each value in decimal.

For addition, five body units become a flag worth five. Four such flags make twenty: carry one into the next place. Subtraction reverses these exchanges.

Fractions

The vigesimal point separates whole numbers from fractional places. To its right, the places are twentieths, four-hundredths, eight-thousandths, and so on.

Punctuation and elision

Use spaces or the interpunct word separator to separate words. Single and double quotation marks have opening and closing forms.

Nzobe marks vowel elision. In typed text, it comes after the stem and radical and before the surviving vowel. The font combines these into the appropriate form.

A full stop ends a sentence. The numeral markers have separate roles: the decimal marker has one low dot, and the vigesimal marker has two low stacked dots. When typing, Period after a numeral inserts the vigesimal marker; use the explicit Full stop control to end a sentence after a number.
